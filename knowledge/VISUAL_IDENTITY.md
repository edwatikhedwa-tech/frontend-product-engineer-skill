# Visual identity

Visual identity means a coherent, intentional language that helps the product be recognized and understood. It does not mean novelty, decoration, or a fixed color palette.

## Identity test

Write one paragraph that explains the product’s visual language without naming a framework or listing tokens. Then check:

- If the logo and brand name disappear, does the product still have a recognizable point of view?
- Can three signature decisions be named and justified by the product job?
- Are typography, spatial composition, surfaces, navigation, and data presentation intentionally related?
- Does the language support the product’s speed, trust, focus, density, or calm as appropriate?
- Is the identity consistent across a primary screen, a state, and a narrow viewport?
- Is the identity distinctive without becoming decorative or inaccessible?

## Identity evidence

Evidence should include the design direction, rationale for signature decisions, and rendered screenshots of at least one primary state plus one non-happy-path or narrow state. A verbal claim that the result “feels branded” is not sufficient.

## Generic comparison

Ask whether the result could be mistaken for a random AI-generated admin interface built from cards, borders, pills, badges, blue buttons, and default spacing. If yes, fail the anti-generic gate and return to Design Director. If the product intentionally uses conventions for usability, explain which conventions are preserved and where the product’s own language appears.

## Distinctive is not decorative

Distinctiveness may come from editorial typography, a strong spatial rhythm, a useful navigation model, a disciplined surface treatment, information-rich but calm data presentation, meaningful motion, or a characteristic interaction pattern. It must improve recognition or task performance, not merely add visual novelty.
